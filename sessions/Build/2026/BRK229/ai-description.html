AI coding adoption followed a familiar enterprise pattern: The speakers compared today’s generative AI adoption to the early days of Kubernetes and cloud-native technologies, with similar excitement, skepticism, and operational challenges. Think uses AI extensively in real-world coding workflows: Their company, Think, applies AI-assisted development in energy management systems that support over 10,000 commercial and residential buildings, including critical infrastructure. Autonomous AI workflows are used to scale engineering tasks: They described a workflow where product requirements are broken into features and tasks, then handled in parallel by AI agents using Copilot CLI and task orchestration. Early skepticism came from reliability and safety concerns: Because their systems affect heating, ventilation, and power in real buildings, the team was cautious about trusting AI-generated code in safety-sensitive environments. Model improvements created a major turning point: The release of newer AI models significantly improved productivity, turning AI from something frustrating into a genuinely useful development partner. AI created measurable business value beyond engineering: Different teams used AI for practical outcomes, such as building RFQ workflows that reduced turnaround time from days to minutes, with high-value business impact. AI empowered non-traditional builders: Team members outside classic software engineering roles—such as product and business stakeholders—were able to prototype, build workflows, and contribute more directly. AI dramatically reduced external development costs: One example shared was building a company website in-house with AI assistance, saving an estimated $120,000 to $200,000 per year. Vendor dependency became a major operational risk: When Anthropic banned their organization in April 2026, many AI-enabled workflows stopped suddenly, exposing issues like vendor lock-in, lack of portability, and FinOps pressure. The biggest lesson: prepare for disruption and portability: Their conclusion was that AI tools, like human collaborators, can become unavailable at any time—so teams need backup plans, portable workflows, and cost-aware strategies.
